# Exploration: Miura for Crypto & Payments

> **Status: exploration only — NOT a roadmap commitment.** An adversarial
> investigation (Opus 4.8, ~15 domain searches) into whether Miura should
> extend toward smart contracts and/or payments. Its verdict is
> deliberately skeptical. Nothing here is planned work; the roadmap is
> unchanged.

## Executive summary

The crypto/smart-contract angle is seductive and, on inspection, largely a trap. The *rhetorical* fit is excellent — "audit the tiny spec, trust the compiler, contracts are invariants" is almost verbatim what contract auditing wants. But three things undercut it: (1) the space is crowded with well-funded, formally-grounded incumbents (Move + Move Prover, Vyper, Scilla, Certora, Halmos, Kontrol) that already do the safety part *better* than a CRUD-oriented compiler could; (2) compiling to the EVM imports an adversarial-semantics surface and, fatally, an **immutability** constraint that breaks Miura's core iteration and migration loop; and (3) the empirical loss data shows most crypto money is stolen by things a safer contract language does nothing about — access-control/key-management failures, not the invariant violations Miura is built to prevent. The **non-crypto payments-ledger** direction — double-entry backends, idempotency, reconciliation, payment state machines — is a genuinely good, lower-risk fit that sits squarely inside Miura's existing `ledger.miura` competence and needs no adversarial-semantics import. **Recommendation: park crypto, pursue the boring payments-ledger angle.** Confidence: moderate-to-high (~75%).

## 1. Where Miura's model actually maps to contracts

The conceptual overlap is real and worth stating plainly, because it is what makes the idea tempting:

- **Invariants → `require`/`assert`.** Miura's field `(require (>= balance 0))` and action `ensures` are exactly the "balance must never go negative," "totals must be conserved" properties that `require`/`assert` statements guard in Solidity. `examples/ledger.miura` already expresses conservation-of-value (`(ensures (= (+ from_after to_after) (+ from_before to_before)))`) — the single most important property a token contract must hold.
- **Ownership → access control.** Miura's `(allow (role admin))` / `(allow (owner owner))` is a declarative version of Solidity's `onlyOwner`/`msg.sender ==` checks — and access-control bugs are, empirically, the dominant exploit class (below).
- **Atomic multi-effect actions → EVM transaction atomicity.** Miura's "all effects commit or none do" is *already how the EVM works* — a reverted transaction rolls back all state. The transfer in `ledger.miura` maps almost one-to-one onto a token `transfer()`.
- **Determinism → the EVM is deterministic.** Miura's deterministic-compiler bet doesn't fight the target here; the EVM demands determinism.

And the pain is real. Cumulative DeFi hack losses passed **$9.1B** by end-2024; 2025 crossed **$3.1B in the first half alone**. The canonical bugs are invariant/permission failures: The DAO ($60M, reentrancy, 2016), Parity ($150M+ frozen, access-control, 2017). Audits are humans reading Solidity at **$50K–$100K for a standard DeFi protocol, $150K–$500K for bridges/ZK systems**. "Shrink the audit surface to ~100 reviewable lines" is precisely the value proposition auditing is begging for.

**But the fit has a hole at its center.** The loss data says the bugs Miura prevents are *not where the money goes*. In 2024, **access-control vulnerabilities accounted for ~75% of all hacked value**, and **private-key compromise alone cost $408.9M**. The biggest single event of the era, the 2025 Bybit theft, was a signing/operational compromise, not a contract-logic bug. Off-chain incidents were **80.5% of funds lost** in one 2024 accounting. Reentrancy-and-friends — the class Miura's contracts genuinely address — is the *minority* of dollar losses. A safer contract language does nothing about a stolen multisig key. So the honest version of the pitch is "Miura would harden the ~20–40% of losses that are on-chain logic bugs," not "Miura fixes crypto's loss problem." That is a much smaller prize than the headline $9B suggests.

## 2. Solidity/EVM as a compile target — feasibility verdict

Could Miura emit Solidity/bytecode the way it emits Python? Mechanically, yes — a deterministic template emitter over the same bundle AST is not harder than the Python emitter, and the EVM's determinism and atomicity are *friendlier* to Miura's model than SQLite's threading model was (the head-to-head found SQLite threading bugs the compiler eliminates). But the semantics it would have to import are the problem.

**Surmountable with moderate work:**
- **Invariants, ownership, atomic transfers, determinism** — map cleanly, as above.
- **`msg.sender` / `msg.value`** — a modest extension of the existing `@user` and auth model; `@user` is already "the authenticated caller," and `msg.sender` is its on-chain analogue.
- **Reentrancy** — arguably Miura's *strongest* card. Because Miura has no user-written control flow, no arbitrary external calls, and a checks-effects-interactions structure could be enforced *by construction* in the emitter, the compiler could make reentrancy structurally impossible — the way Vyper *tried* to. This is a real, defensible feature.

**Genuine dealbreakers or near-dealbreakers:**
- **Immutability breaks Miura's entire iteration and migration loop.** This is the critical one. Miura's design thesis is that the language *grows from failure reports* and that `miurac migrate` deterministically evolves a live schema. Neither survives contact with a deployed contract. A deployed contract **cannot be redeployed** — its address and state are fixed. Miura's "generated artifacts are cattle, re-emit freely" principle is *false* on-chain: the artifact is a pet you can never replace. Fixing a bug means the proxy-upgrade pattern (delegatecall + fixed storage layout), which is itself a leading source of new vulnerabilities — uninitialized initializers, storage-collision corruption, and a governance key that is now a honeypot. Miura's deterministic migration engine has no clean answer for "migrate state under an immutable, adversarially-observed contract without a storage-layout mistake." This isn't a missing feature; it's a collision with Miura's foundational assumption that compilation is cheap and repeatable.
- **Gas metering / DoS.** Every loop, every aggregate, every unbounded write costs gas and can hit the block limit. Miura's `(count Entity)` / `(sum Entity field)` aggregates — free in SQL — are potentially unbounded on-chain iterations that brick a contract as it grows. Miura would have to add a whole cost-and-bounds discipline (bounded collections, pull-over-push accounting) that has no analogue in its current model.
- **Adversarial callers and external calls.** Miura today assumes a trusted server executes its actions. On-chain, *anyone* can call any function in any order, including re-entering mid-execution, and any external call can be a malicious contract. Miura has no model of untrusted composition, MEV/front-running (transaction ordering is adversarial and profitable to manipulate), or oracle manipulation. Supporting these means importing a large adversarial-semantics vocabulary the language deliberately doesn't have.
- **The compiler-is-trusted bet inverts.** Miura's pitch is "trust the compiler, not the model." But **the compiler itself becomes the single point of catastrophic failure** on-chain — and there is a live cautionary tale: the July 2023 Curve/Vyper hack, where a **bug in the Vyper compiler's reentrancy lock** (not in any contract) drained ~$52–70M across Curve, Alchemix, and others. "A safer language whose compiler you trust" is *exactly* what Vyper was, and its compiler bug caused a nine-figure loss. Miura would inherit this risk with a far less battle-tested, AI-shaped compiler and no ecosystem of KEVM-level machine-checked semantics behind it.

**Verdict: infeasible as core language, and a poor risk-adjusted bet even as a dialect.** The reentrancy story is genuinely attractive, but immutability alone breaks the iteration loop that is Miura's reason for existing, and the compiler-as-trusted-root becomes a liability precisely where the stakes are highest.

## 3. Prior-art reality check — is there real differentiation?

This space is not merely crowded; it is crowded with exactly the idea Miura would be selling, executed by teams with formal-methods pedigrees:

- **Move (Aptos/Sui)** — the strongest incumbent. Its **resource types are linear types**: assets cannot be copied or implicitly discarded, so "value is conserved" is enforced *by the type system*, not by a `require` you have to remember to write. It ships the **Move Prover**, an SMT-backed verifier that proves invariants over *all* inputs pre-deployment, with a type-checker-like UX. This is *stronger* than Miura's runtime-checked contracts — it is compile-time proof, and conservation is structural rather than asserted.
- **Scilla (Zilliqa)** — a principled, Coq-formalized language whose whole design is the **separation of communication from computation**, forcing tail-call-only external calls to make reentrancy reasoning tractable. Notably, Scilla already appeared in Miura's *own* earlier research (Dean Mai's "LLM-hardened DSL" essay, cited in `research/novelty-audit.md`) as prior art for verification-embedded languages.
- **Vyper** — the "security, simplicity, auditability, deliberately restricted feature set" language. Miura's "small surface, no risky features" pitch is *Vyper's exact pitch* — and Vyper's compiler-bug hack shows the pitch alone doesn't save you.
- **Formal-verification tooling layer** — Certora Prover (CVL specs, deductive proof for the highest-value protocols), Solidity's built-in **SMTChecker**, a16z's **Halmos** (symbolic bounded model checker over bytecode), **Kontrol/KEVM** (Foundry + full formal EVM semantics). These let you write invariants and *prove* them against existing Solidity, capturing much of Miura's "contracts as the audit surface" value without asking anyone to adopt a new language.

**What could an AI-authored-from-tiny-spec deterministic compiler add that these don't?** Honestly: very little that is defensible. The one arguable delta is the *authoring* axis — Miura is designed to be written by an LLM from a ~200-line guide with zero training data, whereas Move/Vyper/Certora specs are expert-human artifacts. But (a) that advantage is generic to Miura, not specific to crypto; (b) the domain where you would *most* want expert human eyes and machine-checked proofs — not LLM-authored specs over an unproven compiler — is precisely high-value contracts; and (c) Move already gives you conservation-by-typing *and* a prover, which dominates runtime-checked contracts on the exact axis that matters. **Differentiation verdict: largely cosmetic in the crypto context.** Miura's genuine novelty (deterministic hash-identity compiler + self-carried tests + LLM authorship, per the novelty audit's ~65% finding) does not translate into a crypto-specific edge, because the crypto incumbents beat it on the safety axis that this domain cares about most. Entering here means competing on someone else's strongest ground.

## 4. Payments as the nearer-term, lower-risk fit

This is where the report turns constructive. Strip away the blockchain and the *same* transaction+invariant+audit model fits ordinary money-movement backends — and fits them *better and more safely* than the crypto target, because none of the dealbreakers apply. No immutability (you can redeploy and migrate — Miura's whole loop works). No adversarial callers (trusted server). No gas/DoS. No MEV. The execution model is the one Miura already targets.

And the shape is *exactly* `examples/ledger.miura`: double-entry postings (money always has a source and destination), atomic multi-effect transfers, balance invariants, conservation, aggregate balances. The purpose-built systems in this space validate the shape:

- **TigerBeetle** — a financial-transactions database with **double-entry built in**, every transfer exactly one debit + one credit, immutable history, "money never appears or disappears." This is `ledger.miura`'s conservation invariant as a product.
- **Modern Treasury / Formance** — ledger-as-a-service with **idempotency keys** (prevent double-processing on retry), **reconciliation** (internal ledger vs. bank/PSP data), optimistic-concurrency control, and payment state machines. Modern Treasury alone has powered **$400B+** through its ledger.

**Where a Miura contract-carrying spec layer would add value:** internal fintech tooling and money-movement *backends* — not the card rails, not settlement networks, but the app-level logic on top of them. The things Miura already owns (atomic transfers, balance-never-negative, ownership, uniqueness) plus two additions that are natural, non-adversarial extensions of its existing roadmap: **idempotency keys** (a `(unique)` on an idempotency field is 80% of the mechanism — Miura *already has* `(unique)`), and **payment state machines** (the roadmap's item 5a, "enums + state-dependent requires" for legal transitions, is exactly a payment lifecycle: `initiated → pending → settled → reconciled`, with `requires` guarding illegal transitions).

**Where it would NOT add value / would be wrong to claim:** the card rails themselves, real-time settlement, PCI-scope cardholder-data handling, and anything requiring the throughput of a TigerBeetle (Miura targets SQLite-on-one-box per `ROADMAP.md` non-goals). Miura is not a payments *database* and should not pretend to be one; it is a spec layer for the *business logic* that sits above one. Framed that way — "the invariant-holding backend for internal fintech and money-movement tools" — it is a natural, honest extension of the Tier-1/Tier-2 roadmap (internal tools, then customer accounts/orders), not a new domain at all. It is the ledger example, grown up.

## 5. The complexity verdict — core, dialect, or don't

The real worry: every domain Miura eats grows its guide/spec, and crypto imports a huge adversarial-semantics surface. Distinguishing the three lengths clarifies the answer:

- **Per-app bundle length** (should stay small): a token contract bundle would look much like `ledger.miura` — small. This length is *fine*.
- **Guide/spec length** (the real constraint — it is the LLM's in-context spec): this is where crypto is toxic. To author safe on-chain code, the guide would have to teach msg.sender/msg.value, reentrancy and checks-effects-interactions, gas budgeting and bounded iteration, proxy-upgrade storage-layout rules, MEV/ordering awareness, and external-call trust. That is a large adversarial vocabulary added to a guide that is currently ~350 lines of benign CRUD semantics. It would roughly double the in-context spec and, worse, dilute it with concepts irrelevant to every non-crypto user. **This directly violates Miura's "keep the bundle small, stay schematic" discipline.**
- **Compiler complexity** (can absorb more, it is deterministic and out-of-context): a Solidity emitter itself is tractable. But compiler complexity is not the binding constraint — the guide is.

**So the conclusion is clear:** if crypto ever happens, it must be a **separate target/dialect** with its *own* guide loaded only when authoring on-chain code — never core language, never a section bolted into the shared guide. But even as a dialect, section 2's immutability and compiler-as-trusted-root problems remain, so "separate dialect" makes it *survivable to consider*, not *advisable to build*.

Payments, by contrast, needs **almost no guide growth**: idempotency reuses `(unique)`, state machines are the already-planned enum/transition feature, double-entry is the already-shipped multi-effect transaction. It stays schematic and small. That asymmetry — payments is nearly free on the guide budget, crypto roughly doubles it — is itself the strongest argument for which direction to take.

## Final recommendation

- **Crypto / smart contracts: PARK (leaning AVOID).** Confidence ~75%. The rhetorical fit is real but the substance is against it: immutability breaks Miura's iteration-and-migration loop (its reason for existing), the compiler becomes an unproven trusted root in the one domain where that is catastrophic (Curve/Vyper is the warning), the incumbents (Move's linear resource types + Move Prover, Certora, Halmos, Kontrol) already beat runtime-checked-contracts on the safety axis this domain prizes, and the loss data shows most stolen value comes from access-control/key failures a contract language can't touch. The differentiation is cosmetic here. Revisit only if (a) it is scoped as a strictly separate dialect with its own guide, and (b) a compelling reentrancy-by-construction demo materially outperforms Vyper/Move — a high bar. Do not put it on the roadmap now.

- **Payments (non-crypto money movement): PURSUE, as a natural roadmap extension, not a new domain.** Confidence ~70%. It is `ledger.miura` grown up: atomic double-entry, balance invariants, idempotency (`(unique)`), payment state machines (planned enums/transitions), reconciliation aggregates — all inside Miura's existing execution model, with none of crypto's dealbreakers and near-zero guide growth. Scope it honestly as *the invariant-holding backend for internal fintech and money-movement tooling*, explicitly not the card rails or a TigerBeetle-class database. This is the pragmatic, defensible version of "Miura owns what must not break, and money must not break."

**One-line synthesis:** the crypto angle is a distraction that fights Miura's own foundations on someone else's strongest turf; the boring payments-ledger angle is the real opportunity, and it is barely a detour from the road you are already on.
